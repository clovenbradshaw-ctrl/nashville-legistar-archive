WITNESSETH 
WHEREAS, the parties desire to modify the terms and conditions and to add or delete certain other 
terms and conditions to their original agreement dated July 2014, Metro Contract numbered 345468, 
hereinafter the “CONTRACT”, the parties hereby agree as set forth below: 
 
This amendment effects the following changes to the contract as detailed in Exhibit A, attached hereto: 
 
1. Funding in the amount of $41,500.00 is added to the contract in order to make necessary design 
changes and to cover fabrication and construction costs that are greater than anticipated in 
preliminary planning.

3. Added Iran Divestment Act.  
 
This amendment shall not be binding upon the parties until it has been signed first by the CONTRACTOR 
and then by the authorized representatives of the Metropolitan Government and filed in the office of 
the Metropolitan Clerk.

Exhibit A 
Pricing 
 
The pricing model for this contract is as follows: 
METRO shall pay the artist an all-inclusive fixed fee of $350,000.00 which shall constitute 
full compensation for all services, materials, travel, delivery, insurance, licenses, taxes and 
installation to be furnished under the terms of this Agreement.  
 
1st payment: 
$33,500  
To be paid upon execution of this contract by all parties 
and an appropriate invoice from the ARTIST. 
2nd payment 
$50,000 
To be paid upon approval of final design and an 
appropriate invoice from the ARTIST.  
3rd payment 
$75,000 
To be paid upon receipt of stamped 
engineered/structural drawings (see 1.D.) and receipt 
of appropriate invoice from the ARTIST. 
4th payment: 
$95,000 
To be paid upon 50% completion of fabrication of 
walls, receipt and approval of fountain drawings, proof 
of insurance, and receipt of an appropriate invoice from 
the ARTIST. 
5th payment 
$21,500 
To be paid upon 100% completion of fabrication of 
walls and fountain and audio selections and receipt of 
an appropriate invoice from the ARTIST. 
6th payment: 
$50,000 
To be paid upon completion of all work by the ARTIST, 
including installation and written notice of conditional 
acceptance of the project by METRO, provision of 
maintenance manual, and receipt of an appropriate 
invoice from the ARTIST. 
7th payment: 
$25,000  
To be paid upon receipt of all final project 
documentation, including photographic documentation, 
submissions of releases of claim or claim waivers from 
each subcontractor, supplier or other persons or 
entities who has or might have a claim against 
METRO, as-built drawings and receipt of an 
appropriate invoice from the ARTIST. All punch list 
items must be completed before final payment is 
issued.  Upon inclusion in METRO’s fixed asset and 
insurance records and processing of all payments, 
METRO will issue a written notice of final acceptance 
of the Artwork.

E. Site History/Description 
The artwork will be located in the northwest corner of Public Square Park adjacent to the 
historic Metro Courthouse in downtown Nashville, Tennessee. The Art Deco-style courthouse, 
built in 1936, is the fourth such building to occupy the site. The courthouse’s original public 
square was bounded by 19th-century commercial buildings, now demolished. The construction 
of the Victory Memorial Bridge and James Robertson Parkway in the 1950s along the north end 
of the courthouse further reduced the size of the courthouse square. 
 
Following months of Civil Rights protest, Nashville proclaimed the end of segregation here at the 
courthouse on April 19, 1960. Nearly 3,000 Nashvillians silently marched to the Metro 
Courthouse in one of the largest demonstrations in the city’s history. Mayor Ben West met the 
marchers at the courthouse steps. Diane Nash, a student from Fisk University, asked the mayor 
if he felt it was wrong to discriminate against a person based solely on their race or skin color. 
West answered that he agreed it was wrong. Nash then asked him if he believed that lunch 
counters should be desegregated. West answered, "Yes.” 
 
A plaque) to recognize this event was placed on the front of the Metro Courthouse in 1995 (see 
below. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
The courthouse underwent a major renovation beginning in 2003 and its adjacent surface 
parking lot was converted into a public green space. Completed in 2006, Public Square Park 
features a central lawn, fountains, gardens, public art and plantings resting atop a five-level 
underground parking garage that can hold 1,200 cars. Installed in 2006, one of the park’s 
interpretive panels (see below) explains Nashville’s leading role in ending segregation.

The artwork will be located in an outdoor space directly to the west of the Metro Courthouse 
in Public Square Park (general public art location shaded above in purple). The brick plaza 
(shaded on next page in orange) is located between two sets of steps, steps extending down 
towards Charlotte Avenue on the west (photo 1) and up towards the double doors, not used, 
on the Courthouse’s west end (photo 2). To the north (shaded in blue) and south (shaded in 
green) of this space are landscaped areas and can be used as spaces for public art provided the 
trees and landscaping area footprint remain. Each landscaped area has a grass and mulch 
ground cover. Four black gum trees, planted in 2006, anchor both spaces. These are important 
landscaping elements and part of the original formal plan of the Public Square Park. The 
overall design intent should be maintained as public art is integrated into the site. Brick edging 
around the mulched area serves to keep the mulch from washing out due to excessive rainfall. 
Note: these plans from 2006 do not show the site as built. 
 
Two large historical markers—unrelated to the Civil Rights history— are also located in this 
area. A short and wide marble shaft (in area shaded in pink) is located in one of the elevated 
planters against the Metro Courthouse, south of the steps leading up to the double doors on 
the west end. This marker was dedicated to Revolutionary War veterans by the Daughters of 
the American Revolution in 1910. A slim marble obelisk is currently located in the area shaded 
in blue. This marker was dedicated to the First Methodist Church in Nashville in 1957. Before 
2006, it was located in the area shaded in yellow. It is possible for this marker to be relocated 
back to the yellow shaded area to allow all 3 areas (blue, orange, and green) for public art.

G. Site/Community History 
It was in downtown Nashville that students from the city’s four black colleges and seminaries—
Tennessee State University, Fisk University, Meharry Medical College, and American Baptist 
Theological Seminary—launched a nonviolent protest against segregated lunch counters and 
other public accommodations on February 13, 1960, after three months of preparation and 
planning. The first lunch counter sit-in had taken place in Greensboro, North Carolina earlier 
that month, and now Nashville and the rest of the world anxiously turned their attention to the 
mounting tensions in Nashville. Over 100 student protestors  were arrested on February 27. As 
those students sat in jail, other students continued the quiet lunch counter demonstrations 
while the black community responded with a boycott of downtown Nashville stores beginning 
on April 2. 
 
In the early morning of April 19, 1960, an attacker tossed dynamite into the home of attorney Z. 
Alexander Looby, defense counsel for the students and a leader in the desegregation 
movement. Fortunately, Looby and his wife suffered only minor injuries. This shocking act of 
violence mobilized black and white Nashville, where Looby was known and respected. Within 
hours, an estimated 3,000 Nashvillians assembled to march from Looby’s firebombed home to 
Nashville’s City Hall. There they were met by Nashville Mayor Ben West for one of the most 
powerful exchanges in Nashville history. Surrounded by students who would become national 
leaders in the movement—Marion Barry, James Bevel, Bernard Lafayette, John Lewis, C.T. 
Vivian—Diane Nash, the charismatic student leader from Fisk University, challenged Mayor Ben 
West to end racial violence at lunch counters. Nashville became the first major Southern city to 
desegregate its lunch counters. 
 
The next day the Rev. Martin Luther King, Jr., came to Nashville and spoke at Fisk University. 
During the speech, he praised the organization and the discipline of the Nashville sit-in 
movement. He further stated that he came to Nashville “not to bring inspiration but to gain 
inspiration from the great movement that has taken place in this community.” The sit-in

campaign became a major component of the civil rights movement against racial segregation 
and injustice in the South and elsewhere across the United States. The civility of the sit-ins and 
the focus on student training and coordination in non-violent tactics became known as the 
“Nashville Movement” and served as a model for future demonstrations across the country. 
 
Motivated by this success, several of the Nashville Student Movement activists went on to 
participate in the Freedom Rides, interracial bus rides that traveled through the South to test 
the Supreme Court ruling that declared segregation on interstate travel unconstitutional. The 
first Freedom Ride met a violent attack in Alabama which threatened to stop the rides. 
Undeterred, Nashville students joined the ride from Birmingham to Montgomery on May 20, 
1961. They were met by a crowd of violent white protesters, including women and children. 
Several were severely injured in the attack. The Freedom Ride travelled on to Jackson, 
Mississippi, where the students were arrested in the bus terminal waiting room. As a result of 
the Freedom Rides, US Attorney General Robert Kennedy was able to successfully petition the 
Interstate Commerce Commission to adopt stronger regulations and desegregate interstate 
transportation. 
 
Participants in the Nashville Sit-ins and Freedom Rides had witnessed the bravery of much 
younger students, and their parents, in Nashville just a few years earlier. In September 1957, 
three years after the U.S. Supreme Court declared school segregation laws unconstitutional, the 
public schools of Nashville implemented a "stairstep plan". Starting with a group of first graders 
in 1957, one grade a year was to be added until all twelve grades were desegregated. Nineteen 
black first graders enrolled in eight previously all-white schools. On the first day of school, 
hundreds of white protesters stood outside of the schools with signs, shouting at the students 
and their parents. Overnight, a dynamite explosion destroyed a wing of Hattie Cotton 
Elementary School where one black child had enrolled. Fortunately, support for the protestors 
waned and they backed down. With no further demonstrations, school desegregation quietly 
resumed. There would be major conflicts and crises in the years ahead, but this small victory 
was immeasurable.

Artwork Description 
The following description is of the conceptual design of the artwork. The dimensions 
and processes mentioned here are subject to revision and refinement based on design 
modifications determined by information obtained through public meetings, site 
constraints revealed in drawings and meetings with engineering consultants and Metro 
Parks staff, and fabrication optimizations or choices recommended by fabrication and 
installation consultants. Overall, the piece measure 32’ x 48.5’ Most of the existing 
planting and mulch on the site will be preserved. The paving in the central area will be 
replaced with rough cast-in place concrete paving measuring 32’x32’. The most salient 
constructed elements of the piece will be a series of five 7’6”-tall concrete walls with 
graphic patterns moving from project north to project south. Images from Nashville’s 
Civil Rights Movement will be embedded in the concrete by means of the “graphic 
concrete” method. Additionally, there will be three 3’-diameter by 3’-tall concrete 
reflective fountains distributed among these walls. These fountains will be powered 
either by solar-pumps or connected to the local irrigation system. Two large lights will 
illuminate the space from the east. 
 
Maintenance

existing landscaping, the artwork will require annual re-application of epoxy to the 
concrete surface to maintain sheen. It may also require intermittent removal of dirt and 
leaves from the interior ground surfaces of the space. This description does not apply to 
repairs and restoration of art work caused by vandalism or other unforeseeable 
conditions.

C. WHO IS AN INSURED 
6. Additional Insureds When Required By Written Contract, Written Agreement Or Permit  
The person(s) or organization(s) identified in Paragraphs a. through f. below are additional 
insureds when you have agreed, in a written contract, written agreement or because of a permit 
issued by a state or political subdivision, that such person or organization be added as an 
additional insured on your policy, provided the injury or damage occurs subsequent to the 
execution of the contract or agreement, or the issuance of the permit.  A person or organization is 
an additional insured under this provision only for that period of time required by the contract, 
agreement or permit. 
f. Any Other Party  
(1) Any other person or organization who is not an insured under Paragraphs a. through e. above, 
but only with respect to liability for “bodily injury, “property damage” or “personal and advertising 
injury” caused, in whole or in part, by your acts or omissions or the acts or omissions of those 
acting on your behalf: 
(a) In the performance of your ongoing operations;  
(b) In connection with your premises owned by or rented to you; or  
(c) In connection with “your work” and included within the “products- completed operations 
hazard, but only if 
     (i) The written contract or written agreement requires you to provide such coverage to 
such additional insured; and  
     (ii) This Coverage Part provides coverage for “bodily injury” or “property damage” included 
within the “products-completed operations hazard.  
(2) With respect to the insurance afforded to these additional insureds, this insurance does not 
apply to:   “Bodily injury, “property damage” or “personal and advertising injury” arising out of the 
rendering of, or the failure to render, any professional architectural, engineering or surveying 
services, including:  inspection, or engineering  
 
E.5. Separation of Insureds 
Except with respect to the Limits of Insurance, and any rights or duties specifically assigned 
in this policy to the first Named Insured, this insurance applies:  
a. As if each Named Insured were the only Named Insured; and  
b. Separately to each insured against whom a claim is made or "suit" is brought.

Contract

and non-contributory with the additional insured’s own insurance, this insurance is primary and 
we will not seek contribution from that other insurance. 
 
E.8.b.  Waiver Of Rights Of Recovery (Waiver Of Subrogation)  
If the insured has waived any rights of recovery against any person or organization for all or part 
of any payment, including Supplementary Payments, we have made under this Coverage Part, 
we also waive that right, provided the insured waived their rights of recovery against such person 
or organization in a contract, agreement or permit that was executed prior to the injury or 
damage.
